## 2. Inputs and sourcing (company statements)

`[sourced]` RA25 p.22, p.97, p.129, p.34:
- Main inputs: ==wheat flour== (the "principal raw material"), edible oils and fats, sugar, eggs, and plastics/resins for packaging; also plastic and paper containers (RA25 p.97, p.129).
- Wheat "is generally traded in US dollars" and its price depends on weather, crop output and global supply and demand (RA25 p.97). The company "continuously" enters hedging agreements for the main raw materials (RA25 p.97).
- Packaging: purchases plastic and paper containers from various suppliers and holds hedging agreements on plastic packaging (RA25 p.97). Polyethylene futures/swaps appear in the derivative list (EEFF25 p.73).
- Minority stakes in some of its main egg and sugar suppliers; no single-supplier dependence in any market (RA25 p.97). Related-party raw-material purchases (Beta San Miguel and others) are itemised in RA25 p.167.
- Corporate offices lead negotiation of the main raw materials; plants manage inventories and local orders; FIFO inventory method; most deliveries continuous, some daily (RA25 p.97).
- Sourcing risk: production depends on availability from multiple suppliers in various countries; alternative suppliers may be delayed (RA25 p.34).
- Price-volatility drivers listed: global competition for resources, currency fluctuations, geopolitical conflicts (Ukraine, Hamas-Israel, US-Iran named), adverse weather/climate, regulation, pests, water risk, inflationary pressures and supply-chain, transport and labor problems (RA25 p.22).
- Fuels/energy: natural gas, LP gas and electricity in plants; gasoline and diesel in distribution (RA25 p.22). Fuel prices flow into packaging and transport costs (RA25 p.22).
- Hedge limitations stated: commodity hedging "cannot fully protect" from cost changes (market illiquidity, local rules, quality variation); hedge contracts may lock prices above market (RA25 p.22-23). No assurance the company can pass cost increases on to consumers (RA25 p.22).
- Sugar (RA25 p.23): sugar is a main input; increases "including those from additional contributions or tariffs or duties or restrictions on sugar imports in Mexico" would raise cost of sales and cut operating profit if not passed through. US-origin sugar is exempt from Mexican general import duty if certified as not under the US "Sugar Reexport Program". Mexico's Economy Ministry runs an import quota system for non-USMCA sugar; the 2025-26 cycle (1-Oct-2025 to 30-Sep-2026) export cap to the US is 199,251.799 metric tons raw value, under the 2017 suspension agreement (30% of refined sugar export cap per cycle instead of 53%).
- Pricing: product prices are affected by raw-material and distribution costs, the IEPS excise tax in Mexico and consumer price sensitivity (RA25 p.130). Competitive pressure and private-label competition can limit the ability to raise prices in response to input costs (RA25 p.24).

## 3. Hedging of commodities and energy

`[sourced]` EEFF25 p.62, p.72-73; RA25 p.97, p.149, p.296:
- Policy: hedging only; the **purchasing department** manages commodity price risk and checks open derivative positions against corporate strategy (RA25 p.149). Hedge ratio 1:1; purchases match the hedged highly probable forecast transactions (EEFF25 p.72).
- Instruments: commodity futures, options on futures, input swaps (EEFF25 p.62).
- Commodities hedged: "wheat, the main input, together with natural gas, are some of the commodities" hedged (EEFF25 p.72). Dec-2025 contract list covers diesel, gasoline, natural gas, polyethylene, wheat, corn, soybean oil, petroleum, with maturities from Jan-2026 to Dec-2026 (EEFF25 p.73; contract counts and fair values are partly illegible in the extraction).
- Accounting: designated cash-flow hedges; closed wheat derivatives are held in OCI until the raw material is received and not yet applied to cost of sales at 31 Dec 2025, 2024 and 2023 (EEFF25 p.72).
- P&L effect: amounts charged to cost of sales from commodity hedges were ==647 (2025), 1,053 (2024), 1,767 (2023)== (EEFF25 p.73). FX hedges reclassified to cost of sales: 481, (65), 1,916 (EEFF25 p.72). See [[bimbo-fx-exposure]].
- Fair value: Level 1 (listed markets) (EEFF25 p.73).
- The committee report states that a "Global Commodity Hedging Model" was presented: the strategy is based on comparing the current price with the ==average of the last five years== and on analysis of market conditions and trends (EEFF25 p.9). Hedge coverage percentages are not disclosed.
- Energy supply: electricity contracts with wind-farm owners for multiple plants; 100% renewable electricity in 28 of 35 countries and 97% renewable electricity globally in FY2024 `[sourced]` 4T24 rel. p.2. The 2024 Energy Reform could raise power tariffs and competitiveness is unclear; if suppliers fail, the company would buy at potentially higher rates (RA25 p.50-51). The company is also an energy self-supply buyer with inflation-indexed prices (RA25 p.147). Virtual PPAs (US 12-year from 2018; Canada 15-year from 2021) are fair-valued through the financing cost and drove CIF in 4Q25 and 2Q26 (RA25 p.148; 4T25 rel. p.8; 2T26 rel. p.6).

## 6. Tariffs and trade policy

`[sourced]` RA25 unless noted (RA24 carried the same US-tariff risk text, RA24 p.53):
- **USMCA/T-MEC review**: first joint review "scheduled for July 2026" decides whether the treaty is extended for six more years (current expiry 2036) or a member withdraws (withdrawal "highly improbable"). For 2026 the review is described as the factor of greatest potential impact (RA25 p.52-53).
- **US tariffs (announced 2-Apr-2025)**: only Mexican goods meeting USMCA origin and regional-content rules are exempt; other Mexican goods face a 25% tariff. The text says Mexico ships more than 80% of its exports to the US and that more than about 50% of those exports would fail USMCA origin rules and face 25% (RA25 p.53). Possible retaliation, supply-chain disruption, inflation and a slowdown in Mexico are listed (RA25 p.53-54).
- **Mexican import tariffs**: Decree published 29-Dec-2025 raises tariffs on about 1,463 tariff lines (textiles, footwear, iron/steel, aluminum, auto parts, plastics; chapters 39, 50-63, 72, 73, 87), from 5% to 50%, mostly 35%; not applicable to goods originating in free-trade-agreement countries meeting origin rules. The company says the decree "does not directly impact" the products it imports, but expects further tariff changes that could (RA25 p.54-55).
- **Anti-dumping and safeguards**: Mexico has tightened import oversight; the UPCI is using anti-dumping and safeguard tools; relevant inputs could be targeted (RA25 p.55).
- **Customs-law reform in force 1-Jan-2026**: stricter sanctions (250% and 300% penalties for customs declaration inaccuracies, inventory-control failures, omitted taxes and origin violations under preferential tariff programs) and more audits (RA25 p.46).
- **General trade-restriction risk**: the company "imports a considerable percentage of its inputs", so tariff or non-tariff measures in any operating country would affect operations (RA25 p.51).
- **Nearshoring** described as an opportunity; benefits expected on a scale of "five years or more" (RA25 p.55).
- **Product flows**: Mexico peso strength raised the cost of "product imported from Mexico" in North America (1Q24, 2Q24; 1T24 rel. p.5; 2T24 rel. p.5), which means a cross-border flow of finished product from Mexican plants to US/Canada exists; volumes and tariff exposure are not quantified in the filings read.

## 7. Inflation commentary

`[sourced]`:
- Mexico CPI (INEGI) annual: 4.66% (2023), 4.21% (2024), 3.69% (2025). Banxico cut its policy rate by 300 bp in 2025 to 7.0% (RA25 p.57-58). The company says high inflation means higher costs that may not be passed to consumers (RA25 p.58).
- Brazil: SELIC 11.75% (2023), 12.25% (2024), 15.00% (2025) (RA25 p.58-59). Argentina: cumulative inflation about 211% in 2023, about 118% in 2024, 31.5% in 2025; Argentina is hyperinflationary under IAS 29 since July 2018 (RA25 p.59).
- Releases: "general inflation" cited in NA margin drivers in 1Q24 and 2Q24 (1T24 rel. p.5; 2T24 rel. p.5); 2Q26 gross margin cites a "more challenging inflationary environment" (2T26 rel. p.4).
- Geopolitics and energy: Russia-Ukraine, Middle East, Venezuela-US tensions are flagged as sources of inflation in energy and commodities; the group has suspended Bimbo-brand sales and new investment in Russia (RA25 p.55-56).

## Key Takeaways
- Raw materials line is ==~30% of net sales and ~64% of cost of sales== (FY2025); wages ~20% of cost of sales and rising faster than sales; so gross margin is mainly an input-price and labor story `[calc]` (section 1).
- Wheat flour is the principal input and is mostly USD-traded; the company hedges wheat and natural gas with futures/swaps under 1:1 cash-flow hedge accounting; commodity hedges charged 647 to FY2025 cost of sales `[sourced]` RA25 p.97; EEFF25 p.72-73.
- Commentary turned from "lower raw materials" (1Q24-1Q25) to "higher raw materials" (3Q25, 4Q25, 2Q26); gross margin 52.6% in 2Q26 (-20 bp) `[sourced]` (section 4).
- Tariff/trade risk disclosures centre on the July-2026 USMCA review, a 25% US tariff on non-compliant Mexican goods and Mexico's Dec-2025 tariff decree, which management says does not directly hit its imports `[sourced]` RA25 p.52-55.
- Inflation: Mexico CPI easing (4.66% to 3.69% in 2023-2025) while Argentina (31.5% in 2025) and Brazil rates (15.00%) stay high `[sourced]` RA25 p.58-59.
- Gaps: no input-by-input cost split, no hedge coverage ratio and no tariff-exposure quantification in the filings read.
